Ramblings about Art

1. How to Learn to Draw:

Avoid rote learning. In the context of drawing, this is the mistake of simply memorising the outputs of what is drawn without any fundamental/deep understanding of a subject, such as their underlying shape and proportions.

If this habit is developed, then it becomes difficult to draw flexibly; you can only draw things in the same way that you've memorised them and are unable to portray different angles/perspective. This isn't to say that one should just memorise many outputs, that is again simply rote learning and they wouldn't be developing any understanding of what it is that they're drawing.

So then how do you develop an understanding of a subject?

Analyse its shape and proportions.

These two concepts are the fundamental building blocks in learning how to draw. You learn to draw by seeing, analysing, and understanding these two things. The physical motion of drawing is only a part of it.

Break it down into its Shapes

Every object can be broken down into three fundamental shapes: ellipses, triangles, quadrilaterals, and more deeply, their 3D counterparts: spheroids, cylinders and prisms. By simplifying objects down to these shapes, it becomes easier to understand their structure and their different angles/perspective.

This leads to a fundamental prerequisite of being able to draw/imagine these shapes in any angle/perspective. Without it, a person's spatial reasoning may be too weak for understanding all that is made up of these shapes.

Once this prerequisite is developed, then you have the simple yet powerful tool of breaking up and reducing any object down to their fundamental shapes. Correlate it to what it resembles the closest. For example, a hand, with its palm and fingers, is just a flat quadrilateral prism with cylinders sticking out of it. The fingers are just three cyclinders with each segment slightly reducing in length towards the tip. That fleshy bit before your thumb is a spheroid that lays on the palm with a ~90 degree range of movement around the side of it. Even difficult objects, such as wrinkles/folds in fabric, are just triangles. These are just some examples that demonstrate how one can simplify objects recursively, thus developing a deep spatial understanding of any object. The more you break down an object until an observable limit, then the more you notice and understand it.

In contrast, the naive learner when referencing an object, may simply copy its outline or silhouette. An analogy, for example, is someone tracing around the shape of their open hand on a piece of paper. They are not developing any structural understanding of the hand, but just copying what it looks like. Only an abstraction of the hand is learnt, so when they attempt to draw a hand whenever else, it outputs similar to the open hand that they traced. It would be difficult for them to draw the hand in other positions/angles unless they copy new references again, thus rote learning another weak abstraction.

But by analysing an object through its simplified structure, with any of its parts also broken down to their fundamental shapes, then it becomes easier to derive other angles and positions that an object can be in, without the use of more references. (However, its still important to use references to check whether your derivation is correct/accurate; the point is, is that you are no longer using references for rote learning.)
